Critical Play, Concepts in Video Game Art

    
Games are not just stories or images. They are systems we interact with. Critical play means looking at how those systems shape what players can do, how they behave, and what they experience.
      
 
It also means asking what assumptions and values are built into a game's rules, and how artists can question, expose, or change them. What does the game make possible? What does it prevent? What does it reward? What does it assume? And what happens when we play against those assumptions?



    
Player + Game

    

      

        

          
Affordance
What does the game suggest you can do?
Example: A glowing object suggests that you can pick it up. If the game places a weapon in front of you but does not let you use it, it deliberately breaks that expectation.

          
Agency
How much can your actions meaningfully affect what happens?
Example: A dialogue gives you three different responses, but all three lead to exactly the same outcome. You have choices, but little agency.

          
State
The current condition of the game at a particular moment.
Example: You pick up a key. The game changes key = false to key = true. A locked door can now open. Your action changed the game state.

        

      

    


    
Systems + Behavior

    

      

        

          
Emergence
Something happens because several simple systems interact, rather than because the designer explicitly scripted that event.
Example: Fire spreads to a wooden bridge → the bridge burns down → an NPC can no longer cross → the NPC finds another route. Each rule is simple, but together they produce an unexpected situation.

          
Procedurality
Games can communicate ideas through rules and processes, not only through images or stories.
Example: A game about capitalism might require the player to constantly accumulate money to pay increasing debts. The idea is expressed through what the player has to do, rather than through dialogue explaining capitalism.

          
Simulation
A rule-based model of something. Every simulation decides what to include, simplify, or leave out.
Example: A game models an ecosystem using only three variables: plants, herbivores, and predators. Changing one population affects the others.

        

      

    


    
Meaning + Politics

    

      

        

          
Representation
How people, places, objects, or ideas are depicted.
Example: A game shows a city neighborhood as abandoned, dangerous, and full of crime. Those visual and narrative choices shape how that place and its inhabitants are represented.

          
Representation vs. Simulation
A game represents pollution by showing a polluted river.
A game simulates pollution when factories produce pollution, pollution accumulates over time, fish die, and the player's actions can change the outcome.
A game can do both.

          
Games as Political Systems
Rules can encode values:
            
    
              
  • What gets rewarded or punished?
    • 
              
  • Who has agency?
    • 
              
  • What resources are available, and to whom?
    • 
              
  • What behaviors does the system encourage?
    • 
              
  • What possibilities are excluded?
    • 
            

            Games can model systems such as labor, housing, borders, economies, policing, migration, inequality, or care.
They can also make arguments through their mechanics. This is central to persuasive and activist games: the rules themselves can communicate a political position.

        

      

    

  


  

    
Player + Game

    
Affordance

    
Affordance: What does the game suggest I can do?

    
An object's appearance, location, context, and our previous experience with the game help us understand how we might interact with it.

    
A door might suggest:

    
    
      
  • Enter if it is open.
    • 
      
  • Open if it has a handle.
    • 
      
  • Unlock if it has a visible lock and I have a key.
    • 
      
  • Break if it looks damaged and I am carrying a weapon.
    • 
      
  • Come back later if none of my current abilities work.
    • 
      
  • Nothing if I have learned that similar doors are just decoration.
    • 
    

    
Affordance is therefore not only about the object itself. It depends on the relationship between the object, environment, player abilities, game rules, and context.

    
A mountain in the distance might look reachable, but whether I understand it as somewhere I can actually go depends on what the game has taught me about its world.

    
Question: What does the game make me think is possible?

    
Agency

    
Agency: What kind of power does the player have to act within the game?

    
Agency is more than simply having choices. It comes from the relationship between the player's goals, abilities, constraints, choices, and the consequences of those choices.

    

      
“The rules and goals of games are not arbitrary at all. They are actually a way of specifying particular modes of agency for the player to adopt.”

      
C. Thi Nguyen, “Games and the Art of Agency,” The Philosophical Review 128, no. 4 (2019), p. 423

    

    
Nguyen argues that game designers don't just create a world. They design a particular way of acting within that world.

    
They decide:

    
What can I do?
What am I trying to accomplish?
What resources and abilities do I have?
What constrains me?
What counts as success or failure?

    
Take the locked door again. I might be able to:

    
unlock it
break it
pick the lock
convince someone to open it
find another route

    
But those possibilities depend on who I am within the system.

    
Do I have the key?
Am I strong enough to break it?
Did I learn lock-picking?
Does the guard trust me?
Am I even allowed to decide where I go?

    
The same environment can therefore produce very different forms of agency for different players or characters.

    
Agency also doesn't necessarily mean freedom. Restricting agency can be part of the meaning of a game. A game might deliberately make you powerless, force you to choose between bad options, or require you to operate within an oppressive system.

    
Salen and Zimmerman connect agency to consequences:

    

      
“Meaningful play in a game emerges from the relationship between player action and system outcome.”

      
Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman, Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals (2004), Chapter 3, “Meaningful Play”

    

    
So agency isn't simply:

    
How many choices do I have?

    
It is:

    
What am I able to do, what am I trying to do, what constrains me, and how does the game respond to my actions?

    
State

    
Game state: The current condition of the game and its systems.

    
State is what allows a game to remember what has happened and use that information to determine what can happen next.

    
A door might have:

    
locked = true

    
The player might have:

    
hasKey = false

    
After finding the key:

    
hasKey = true

    
The door hasn't necessarily changed, but the relationship between the player and the door has.

    
State can include:

    
health = 50
doorOpen = false
enemyAlive = true
timeOfDay = night
relationship = 8
questCompleted = false

    
These states can interact:

    
hasKey = true + doorLocked = true → player can unlock door

    
ACTION → STATE CHANGE → NEW POSSIBILITIES

  


  

    
Systems + Behavior

    
Emergence

    
Emergence: Complex or unexpected situations that arise when simpler rules and systems interact.

    
Imagine a game where:

    
fire spreads
wood burns
rain extinguishes fire
NPCs avoid danger
bridges can collapse

    
The designer doesn't need to explicitly program:

    
“The player can burn the bridge to stop an enemy from following them.”

    
That possibility can emerge from the interaction between the existing systems.

    
Salen and Zimmerman describe emergence as:

    

      
“Systems generating complex and unpredictable patterns of behavior from simple rules.”

      
Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman, Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals (2004), Chapter 14, “Games as Emergent Systems”

    

    
SIMPLE RULES + INTERACTION → COMPLEX BEHAVIOR

    
The designer creates the conditions for possibilities without necessarily scripting every event that can occur.

    
Emergence can happen between:

    
game systems
fire + weather + physics

    
players and systems
players discovering an unintended strategy

    
players
alliances, competition, cooperation, social norms

    
This is one reason games can produce experiences their designers did not completely predict.

    
Procedurality

    
Procedurality: How does the game produce things through rules and processes?

    
Games don't just contain objects, images, and stories. They define relationships and processes between things.

    
For example:

    
player works → money increases
time passes → rent increases
player rests → health increases
player works → health decreases

    
Once these processes interact, they create a system the player has to navigate.

    
Bogost defines processes as:

    

      
“The methods, techniques, and logics that drive the operation of systems.”

      
Ian Bogost, Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames (2007), Chapter 1, “Procedural Rhetoric”

    

    
Procedurality shifts our attention from:

    
What is in the game?

    
to:

    
How does the game work?

    
Procedural Rhetoric

    
Processes can also express ideas and make arguments.

    

      
“I call this new form procedural rhetoric, the art of persuasion through rule-based representations and interactions rather than the spoken word, writing, images, or moving pictures.”

      
Ian Bogost, Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames (2007), Preface

    

    
Imagine a game where:

    
rent ↑
wages stay the same
work hours ↑
free time ↓
health ↓

    
The game doesn't need a character to tell us:

    
“This economic system makes life increasingly difficult.”

    
The player experiences that relationship by operating inside the system.

    
Procedural rhetoric asks us to look at what the rules make:

    
possible / impossible
easy / difficult
valuable / worthless
rewarded / punished
visible / invisible

    
Question: What argument do the game's rules make?

    
Simulation

    
Simulation: A rule-based model of how something behaves.

    
A simulation does not need to reproduce reality perfectly. It selects which properties, behaviors, and relationships matter.

    
A tree could simply be scenery.

    
Or the game might simulate:

    
tree needs water
tree grows over time
tree burns
tree produces wood
tree provides habitat

    
The designer decides which of these relationships exist and which do not.

    
Frasca defines simulation as:

    

      
“To simulate is to model a (source) system through a different system which maintains [...] some of the behaviors of the original system.”

      
Gonzalo Frasca, “Simulation versus Narrative: Introduction to Ludology,” in The Video Game Theory Reader (2003)

    

    
This means every simulation is selective.

    
A farming game might simulate crop growth but not soil depletion.

    
A city simulation might model taxes and employment but not race.

    
A war game might model ammunition and territory but not civilian trauma.

    
Those choices shape what kind of world the simulation produces.

    

      
“The key trait of simulational media is that it relies on rules: rules that can be manipulated, accepted, rejected, and even contested.”

      
Gonzalo Frasca, “Simulation versus Narrative: Introduction to Ludology,” in The Video Game Theory Reader (2003)

    

    
Question: What does the simulation model, and what does it leave out?

  


  

    
Meaning + Politics

    
Representation

    
Representation: How does the game depict or present something?

    
Representation can happen through:

    
images
characters
environments
language
sound
narrative
symbols and categories

    
A city might be represented as:

    
dangerous
wealthy
abandoned
crowded
utopian

    
Representation therefore asks us to examine how people, places, objects, identities, events, and ideas are portrayed.

    
But in games, representation can interact with simulation.

    
A neighborhood might look dangerous, while the game's systems also define:

    
crime rate = high
police presence = high
property value = low
NPC hostility = high

    
Now the idea isn't only communicated through images or narrative. It is also built into how the world behaves.

    
Representation vs. Simulation

    

      
Representation

      
What does the game show or describe?

      
A game depicts a polluted river:

      
dirty water
dead fish
industrial buildings
dark colors

      
Simulation

      
How does the system behave?

      
factory production ↑
↓
pollution ↑
↓
water quality ↓
↓
fish population ↓
↓
player closes factory
↓
pollution ↓

      
A game can therefore communicate through both what it depicts and how its systems behave.

    

  


  

    
How the Concepts Connect

    
Connecting the concepts through the example of the door

    
Take one locked door:

    

      

        

          
AFFORDANCE
What seems possible?
The door looks locked. Maybe I need a key, another entrance, or a way to break it.

          
AGENCY
What am I capable of doing?
Can I unlock it, break it, pick the lock, convince someone to open it, or walk away?

          
STATE
What is currently true?
doorLocked = true
hasKey = false
guardTrust = 20
Those conditions determine what can happen.

          
PROCEDURALITY
What rules determine how the door works?
Maybe: hasKey = true → unlock or strength > 8 → break

          
EMERGENCE
Can interacting systems create another possibility?
Maybe fire spreads from another room and destroys the door without the designer explicitly scripting that solution.

          
SIMULATION
What properties of a door does the game model?
Can doors lock? Burn? Break? Make noise? Be repaired? Who is allowed to open them?

          
REPRESENTATION
How is the door presented?
Does it look secure, fragile, forbidden, institutional, domestic, inviting, threatening?

        

      

    

  


  

    
Critical Play

    
Understanding critical play

    

      
“Critical play means to create or occupy play environments and activities that represent one or more questions about aspects of human life.”

      
Mary Flanagan, Critical Play: Radical Game Design (2009), p. 6

    

    
Critical play treats games as spaces where rules, conventions, values, and assumptions can be questioned.

    
Because games are systems, critique can happen not only through a game's story or imagery, but through playing with, against, or changing its rules.

    

      
“Because they primarily exist as rule systems, games are particularly ripe for subversive practices.”

      
Mary Flanagan, Critical Play: Radical Game Design (2009), p. 11

    

    
Critical question: What happens when we challenge what the game expects us to do?

    
Now we can look at the entire system critically:

    
Why can I break the door but not knock?

    
Why can I fight the guard but not negotiate with them?

    
Why does the game reward entering this space?

    
Who decided what counts as success?

    
Which actions are impossible?

    
Which behaviors are rewarded or punished?

    
What does the simulation model and what does it ignore?

    
What assumptions about the world are encoded in these rules?

    
And finally:

    
What happens if we resist, misuse, subvert, or redesign them?

  


  

    
Events

    
An event tells Unity that something happened.

    
Events usually start a piece of game logic:

    
On Start → Show Instructions

On Trigger Enter → Start Dialogue

On Collision Enter → Play Sound


    
Common Events

    

      

        

          
Start
Runs once when the GameObject starts.

          
Update
Runs every frame. Use it for something that needs to be checked continuously.

          
Input Event
Runs when the player performs an input, such as pressing Interact or Jump.

          
On Trigger Enter / Stay / Exit
Runs when something enters, stays inside, or leaves a trigger area.

          
On Collision Enter / Stay / Exit
Runs when physical objects make, maintain, or end contact.

        

      

    

    
An event answers:

    
When should this logic run?

  


  

    
Detection

    
An event can tell us something happened, but we often need to know what caused it.

    
For example, On Trigger Enter means:

    
Something entered this trigger.

    
It does not automatically mean:

    
The player entered this trigger.

    
On Trigger Enter provides Other, a reference to the Collider that entered.

    
On Trigger Enter
↓
Other
↓
Check Tag: Player

    
Now the game can respond only when the Player enters.


    
Trigger or Collision?

    
Both use Colliders, but they detect different things.

    

      

        

          
Trigger
Use a Trigger when you want a Collider to act as a sensor or area without physically blocking objects.
Enable: Collider → Is Trigger ✓

Good for:
entering a room
approaching an NPC
reaching a checkpoint
entering water

          
Collision
Use a Collision when the physical contact itself matters.

Good for:
ball hits wall
player lands
car hits obstacle
projectile hits enemy

        

      

    

    
Player enters Trigger
↓
On Trigger Enter
↓
Show Message

Ball hits Wall
↓
On Collision Enter
↓
Play Impact Sound

    
Trigger = detect an area

    
Collision = detect physical contact

    
A door could use both: a Collider physically blocks the player, while a larger Trigger detects when the player is close enough to interact.


    
Other Types of Detection

    

      

        

          
Input
Detect something the player does:
Interact
Jump
Move
Click

          
Raycast
A Raycast sends an invisible line through the game world and reports what it hits.

Use it to ask:
What am I looking at?
What did the gun hit?
Is there ground below me?

          
Distance
Compare how far apart two objects are:
Distance(Player, Enemy) < 5

Use this when proximity matters but you don't need a specific trigger area.

        

      

    

  


  

    
Conditions

    
Sometimes detecting something isn't enough. The game needs to check whether something is true before deciding what to do.

    
On Trigger Enter
↓
Is this the Player?
↓
Branch
 ↙    ↘
TRUE  FALSE

    
A condition produces a Boolean value:

    
true
false

    
A Branch uses that value to choose which flow to follow. It is the Visual Scripting equivalent of an if statement.

  


  

    
State and Variables

    
Now imagine a locked door.

    
Knowing that the player interacted with the door isn't enough. The game also needs to remember:

    
Does the player have the key?

    
This information is part of the game's state.

    
State is stored in variables:

    
bool hasKey = false;
int health = 100;
float speed = 4.5f;
string playerName = "Alex";


    

      

        

          
Boolean
bool stores either true or false.
Useful for two-state information:
bool hasKey = true;
bool doorOpen = false;
bool enemyAlive = true;

          
Integer
int stores whole numbers:
int health = 100;
int score = 250;

          
Float
float stores numbers with decimals:
float speed = 4.5f;
float distance = 3.25f;
In C#, a float literal needs f.

          
String
string stores text:
string playerName = "Alex";
string message = "The door is locked.";

        

      

    


    
State Can Change

    
Variables let the game remember what has happened.

    
Before picking up a key:

    
bool hasKey = false;

    
After picking it up:

    
bool hasKey = true;

    
Later, the game can check:

    
hasKey == true
↓
Branch
 ↙    ↘
TRUE  FALSE
 ↓      ↓
Open   Locked
Door   Message

    
== means compare two values.

    
= means set a value.

    
This distinction becomes especially important when we move into C#.

  


  

    
Building an Interaction

    
A complete interaction combines these pieces.

    
Locked Door

    
EVENT
Player presses Interact
↓
DETECTION
Raycast: What is the player looking at?
↓
CONDITION
Is it a Door?
↓
STATE
Get Variable: hasKey
↓
BRANCH
Is hasKey true?
 ↙          ↘
TRUE        FALSE
 ↓            ↓
Open Door    Show "Locked"
↓
STATE CHANGE
doorOpen = true

    
This gives us a basic structure that can be reused for many game systems:

    
EVENT → DETECTION → CONDITION → ACTION → STATE CHANGE

    
Not every interaction needs every step. A simple trigger might only be:

    
On Trigger Enter → Play Sound

    
A more complex interaction adds detection, conditions, and stored state only when they are needed.
